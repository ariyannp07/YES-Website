# 1. Overview

YES should feel like entering an active room of builders at Yale: ambitious, useful, and already in motion. The system uses an ink-black navy ground, institutional Yale blue, restrained brass signals, documentary portraits, direct language, and visible evidence of work. Pages share one shell and one rhythm so the globe, directory, press, and programs feel like parts of the same organization.

# 2. Colors

- Yale blue (`#00356B`) is the committed brand field for navigation, high-emphasis sections, and primary identity.
- Deep blue (`#001F3F`) adds depth when a darker field is needed.
- Ink-black navy (`#02070D`) is the default ground, with raised surfaces in `#06111E` and `#081522`.
- Muted brass (`#D1B97E`) marks action, current navigation, focus, and honest status labels. It is used sparingly.
- Cool white (`#F0F3F5`) is the primary text color, with muted copy in `#90A2B2`.
- The WSJ clipping alone uses a warm paper surface to distinguish the outside publication from the YES interface.
- Color never substitutes for readable labels; contrast must meet WCAG 2.2 AA.

# 3. Typography

Archivo is the working family for body copy, controls, statistics, and most headings. Times anchors the Yale identity, launch statement, and Thesis, while Bodoni Moda is reserved for the Wall Street Journal clipping. These display faces are deliberate exceptions rather than a second interface system. Bold, tightly set headings give the organization a decisive voice; body copy remains open and practical. Display letter spacing never exceeds `-0.04em`, the largest type stops at `6rem`, and paragraph measures remain close to 70 characters. Labels use weight and concise wording instead of decorative all-caps repetition.

# 4. Elevation

The site is primarily flat and editorial. Hierarchy comes from scale, color fields, spacing, borders, and image crops rather than shadows. Overlays may use one restrained backdrop and a clearly elevated panel. Rounded corners are modest—typically 6 to 16 pixels—and should never make every section look like a floating card.

# 5. Components

- The top bar is hidden on the initial launch and appears after the visitor begins scrolling. It exposes Thesis, People, Common Room, Press, and Join YES.
- The launch globe is a rotatable 3D field of geographic lights. New Haven and San Francisco are labeled, and its reveal completes in under a second.
- The Wall Street Journal hook acts as third-party proof and is treated like a small newspaper clipping, not a generic site card.
- The homepage uses an anonymous, staggered portrait composition; names belong in the directory. Directory cards lead with consented portraits and use intentional monograms as honest fallbacks.
- Pending initiatives use the brass status marker. Common Room deliberately communicates only its name and pending state until real details exist.
- Buttons are solid, rectangular, and action-led. Links remain recognizable and all interactive elements have visible keyboard focus.

# 6. Do’s and Don’ts

Do lead with people, proof, and clear verbs. Do keep routes and navigation names literal. Do let sections breathe and preserve the same shell across every page. Do provide reduced-motion behavior and usable 320-pixel layouts.

Don’t hide content behind a cinematic intro. Don’t use generic startup gradients, glass panels, gradient text, oversized decorative type, or repeated eyebrow labels. Don’t manufacture portraits, dates, or program availability. Don’t let motion, canvas, hover, or color carry essential meaning alone.
